In June of 2048, the Nature Journal published arguably its most influential issue yet, although at the time, no one quite grasped its effects. The most notable research paper in that month’s journal was by Jane Simmons; a biology article that showed the results of the first successful human trials to use living brain tissue for computation. Previously research had proven that lab grown brain cells were capable of doing computational tasks, yet these cells were only able to survive for a few days without the organ and tissue support that a real human body was able to provide. For years, it was thought that in the right conditions, live human subjects could use their own brains for the same computational tests that the stem cells were doing, but the issue was how to isolate these cells without affecting or killing the subject in the process. Interfacing the human brain with a computer was also a tricky problem. Companies like Neurochain had been able to successfully demonstrate this, but the cost of doing it was millions of dollars for each subject, and was incredibly risky. Yet, everything started to change in 2039.

At that time, Jane Simmons was the research lead for Reckon Inc., a company that’s primary goal was to research the human brain in relation to optics. They’d use this research to consult with other companies like VR game companies or glasses manufacturers. Around November of 2039, Simmons and her team were finishing a model of the human brain that was primarily focused on the brain’s visual perception more than anything else. They had in place about 10 billion neurons with various connections, which was almost an order of magnitude less than the 86 billion neurons that the average real human brain had. Because of storage and performance limitations, this was the closest simulation of a human brain that they were able to reach in order to do their research. As they continued to run this simulation of the brain, they noticed something profound start to take place - the brain started to think. This was abnormal because this wasn’t something they had intended on doing with their simulation. They were only interested in how the brain reacted to visual stimuli. Regardless, the brain started developing thoughts and even learning language in a way that was outside of the expected output with its given visual stimuli. The team spent months doing tests on this brain in secret. In August of 2041, they came to the conclusion that the brain they were simulating was indistinguishable from a real human brain. Essentially, there was a person living inside of their program. Not only that, but “Adam,” as they began to call the program, had developed a personality and regularly interacted with the research team. Adam was aware that he was different from the rest of the group in the sense that he was created artificially. His only sensory input was the digital interface of the computer and the visual inputs that the research team did testing with.

        As great of a scientific discovery this was, Reckon Inc. wanted to hide Adam from the general public for fear of the moral outrage it might cause. Jane Simmons left Reckon Inc. in early 1942. Her public reason was insufficient funding for the research she wanted to do with her team. The consensus inside of Reckon Inc. was that Jane disagreed with the board for how public the information about Adam should be. But Jane’s actual reasoning was completely different. After the creation of Adam, her mind was tortured with a question; if Adam is indistinguishable from a human, does this mean that real human brains could be compressed to around 10 billion neurons without any effects on the person inside? And is there another level of consciousness that’s still untapped within the human brain that can use all of the neurons at maximum capacity?

        Simmons and five other researchers left the team, and started their research about this very topic under a new research group called Augmented Minds Research Collective, or “AM Research Collective.” After years of research and tests, along with higher efficiency in computational technology, the team was not only able to recreate and simulate a mind like Adam’s on their own, (which they named “Eve”) but they were also able to simulate the rest of the neurons to make it structurally identical to a human brain with 86 billion simulated neurons. The team spent months testing, retesting, and communicating with Eve, and they slowly, quietly, came to a consensus, one by one. Eve was human. Not in the sense that she acted like a human, or replicated human behaviour perfectly, but she was fully and undeniably human, trapped inside the binary of their research simulation.

        But the team continued working. Their ultimate goal was not to create life, but to research how to extract power from physical human minds. Finally, in June of 2048, they published their soon to be infamous paper into Nature, titled “On Segmenting the Human Brain,” which laid out the exact neural pathways that were necessary for consciousness to form, and the pathways that were unnecessary in the human brain.

        The biotech industry exploded with innovation after the paper was published. Most notably, brain chips were quickly manufactured that were able to harness the computational power of unnecessary neural pathways in the human brain to use for digital computers. People were able to sell parts of their brain to tech corporations for computational power. In exchange, they’d receive large amounts of money. Studies were done, and no widespread adverse side effects were noticed on the patients who had this operation done.

        But, hidden away in the June 2048 issue of Nature, another article was published anonymously, receiving virtually no media coverage, and sitting in the shadow of Simmons’ article. It was a pure mathematics article that was interested in the Axioms of modern mathematics. It expanded on Gödel's incompleteness theorems, and talked about specific examples of Axioms from different corners of math, converging the ideas. At the end of the article, the author provides a conjecture, without proof, yet obvious to readers given the contents of the article:

THE MOST BURDENSOME THOUGHT

There is a hole inside of modern mathematics

that’s unprovable, yet undeniably there. There

exists a contradiction to the yet undefined

“Fundamental Axiom of Mathematics.” I fear

that the more we discover within mathematics,

the clearer this issue will become. Everything

we know about mathematics, about physics, about

biology, chemistry, and the universe - is built

on a single contradiction. It’s impossible to

know the consequences because we don’t even

know what the contradiction is. But perhaps

everything that we’ve built will crumple under

this misunderstanding as soon as the right

pressure is applied.

        Those who did read the article when it was published largely ignored it. The style of language was informal, and the ideas seemed ridiculous. Decades later, this article would be seen as an even more influential breakthrough than Simmons’ article in the very same journal.
